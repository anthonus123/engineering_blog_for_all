Why Chaos Is Not Your Enemy

April 8, 2023 · Engineering

An unfortunate sequence of events

“Although the chaos of this room, there is order within disorder”, was once said by my nonlinear dynamics and chaos professor during my undergrad in his poignant French accent. For context, it was the defense used when his mother would complain about the mess that was his room. Little did I know this bold statement would set the tone for a class that would change my perspective on the world. But before diving into the details of the class, let’s set the stage

Around May 2012, my friends started to receive their acceptance for their university applications. I applied to Polytechnique Montreal in Aerospace (1st choice) and Biomedical engineering (2nd choice), and at McGill in Mechanical (3rd choice) and Electrical Engineering (4th choice). At that time, the preferred method of announcing it was through a Facebook status (I know, it sounds so archaic today). In general, I am quite laid back and don’t stress much, so when I learned that my friends that had lower grades than me got accepted in Aerospace Engineering at Polytechnique, I didn’t think much of it. I started telling myself “Oh, Polytechnique receives so many applications, so it’s normal it takes time. Maybe they send out their applications in alphabetical order.” Two weeks passed by, and I still did not receive any updates, so I decided to call. I was surprised how quickly they answered my phone call.

“Did you guys receive my application?”

“No, we did not. Can you double check your application on our website?”

When I validated my application, I noticed to my great dismay that I made a mistake in my permanent code (which is the key to access my grades) by one digit. So I immediately fixed it, and the next day I get an acceptance letter in the Mechanical Engineering program at Polytechnique, which I did not apply for.  When I later inquired about my applications, I was informed the application period was done, and was instead placed in Polytechnique’s mechanical engineering programs. And thus, I opted to go to McGill University in mechanical engineering.

It’s natural to wonder ‘What if? Who would have I met? What experiences would I have had?’ but, it’s impossible to accurately predict how my life would have turned out if that typo never happened. In this blog post, I argue that dwelling on these hypothetical scenarios is futile and a waste of time. And this brings us back to : “Although the chaos of this room, there is order within disorder”. You may wonder, how does chaos relate to the story you just told me? And how does it relate to math, a science governed by deduction and logic?

Chaos and math?

As we progress through our mathematical education, the problems we encounter become increasingly involved. In grade school, we were introduced to basic arithmetic by solving problems such as “What is 2+2?”, and “If Sally has 5 apples and she eats 1, how many does she have remaining?”. These problems have one correct answer and are designed to help us develop our problem-solving skills.

As we move on to middle school, we delve deeper into the world of mathematics by studying algebra (and other interesting topics such as probabilities, statistics, etc.). In algebra, we encounter problem statements such as “Tim is twice John’s age. Together, their ages add up to 15, what are their ages?” These equations require us to solve for ‘x’ and ‘y’ and represent a more advanced level of problem-solving.

In high school (CEGEP in Quebec), students typically take calculus, the science of continuous change. One branch of calculus is called differential equations. These equations arise naturally from studying physics of a system. For example, if you throw a rock up in the air and you know the forces acting on the rock and its initial velocity, you can quite accurately predict the rock’s future position and velocity across time. Therefore, when studying differential equations, we’re looking for functions of one or multiple variables which can describe how a system evolves in time. In the rock throw example, if we’re interested in knowing the height of the rock as a function of time, we’re looking for h(t).

Some dynamical systems exhibit chaotic behaviors, and their properties are quite interesting.  The video below shows the first chaotic system that students are usually exposed to: a double pendulum, which is essentially two rods linked to each other, and both rods are allowed to freely rotate. Before starting the video, try to think what the trajectory of the end of the second bar would look like.

As you may have noticed, it was very difficult to predict the position of the pendulum. Let’s try the same exercise again, but this time with two pendulums. Again, take a moment to predict the movement of the two pendulums.

One property of chaotic systems is their sensitivity to initial conditions. Small changes in their initial conditions can yield very different behaviors for two identical dynamical systems.

Chaos is everywhere

Ok, that is enough about the double pendulum! A system that you’re certainly familiar with is weather systems. Maybe you’re like me, and you don’t rely too much on the prediction given by your local weather station? Well, it’s because you subconsciously already know that weather systems are inherently chaotic. How can you know exactly the temperature, humidity, air density, wind conditions, etc.? As we’ve seen in example the from the double pendulum video, very small changes in initial conditions can have large change in the behavior of the system. Perhaps you’ve heard of the butterfly effect? It’s this popular saying where minor wind perturbations caused by a butterfly flapping its wings can cause a tornado in the future. Well, you can’t rule out this possibility because weather systems are inherently chaotic!

We discussed a bit about chaos in mathematics, but how does this apply to our lives? Why should we care? In some sense, we can think of life as a chaotic system, in the mathematical sense. A series of small actions today can have large consequences in the future (think about my university application, for example). We all have our “what if’s”, and I think knowing that chaos is indistinguishable from life is beautiful. Hear me out: whenever you find yourself in a “what if” loop, you must remember the butterfly effect. No matter how hard you try, you will never be able to accurately predict the future. So dwelling on the past and trying to accurately guess how your life would have turned out is probably not the best use of your time.

The flip side of that is also beautiful. It is because you cannot accurately predict the future that small steps matter a lot as they compound. For example, if you go to the gym today, and you look yourself in the mirror, you will see no visible improvement to your physique. If you go tomorrow, you will also not see anything. However, if you go for a long enough period of time, then you will see the rewards, and other consequences that you cannot predict from the inception of the gym project. Maybe you will make new friends? Maybe the newfound discipline of working out regularly transposes in other areas of your life where you procrastinate too much?

Have you ever found yourself in a situation where you say “no” because either you’re scared or because you “know already what is going to happen, so why even go to your event? ”   Whenever you find yourself in this thought, just remember that your model of the world is imperfect, and most likely your prediction will be inaccurate, so allow yourself to try new things!
